San Francisco Got Let Off the Hook Under CJR. Under TEAM, It Became California's Biggest Market.

Los Angeles stayed a mandatory market under an older Medicare bundled payment program for nine straight years, specifically because it was expensive. San Francisco got downgraded out of that same program in twenty eighteen. Then Medicare ran an entirely separate lottery for its newest program, and the two cities landed on opposite ends of it. Los Angeles drew exactly one hospital, and that hospital is there by choice. San Francisco drew thirty seven, and every one of them is required to be there.

Start with what came before. From twenty sixteen through the end of twenty twenty-four, Medicare ran a mandatory bundled payment program for hip and knee replacement called CJR, in sixty seven metro areas chosen by lottery. California had three of those areas: Los Angeles, San Francisco, and Modesto. All three started out mandatory. Two years in, Medicare cut the mandatory list down to the thirty four markets with the highest historical spending on these surgeries, not by drawing again, but by rank. Los Angeles spent enough to stay on that list. San Francisco and Modesto didn't, and both dropped to voluntary.

Six years after that, Medicare designed a new program, called TEAM, and ran its own separate lottery to decide which markets would be required to join. This lottery had nothing to do with CJR. It used its own criteria and its own draw. But look at what happened to these same three California markets anyway.

Los Angeles has one hospital in the new program, Cedars-Sinai, and it's there because it volunteered, not because it was required to.

San Francisco has thirty seven hospitals in the new program, more than any other market in California, and every one of them is required to participate.

Modesto isn't in the new program at all.

So the market Medicare once forced to stay in for nine straight years, because it was too expensive to let go, ended up with the smallest possible footprint in the next program, entirely voluntary. And one of the markets Medicare let off the hook early became the single largest mandatory market in the state.

That's a striking coincidence involving three cities. The real question is whether it's actually a pattern, or just three cities being unusual. So widen the lens from three markets to all sixty seven of the original ones.

Medicare doesn't publish a clean, current list of exactly which thirty four markets stayed mandatory after twenty eighteen. So this analysis rebuilt that list, by applying the same rule Medicare said it used at the time, highest historical spending per episode, to the original sixty seven markets, using Medicare's own spending data from the earlier rule. Before trusting that rebuilt list for anything, it was checked against the three things already known for certain: does it correctly show Los Angeles staying mandatory, and San Francisco and Modesto dropping to voluntary? It does. All three came out exactly as documented.

With that list in hand, here's what happened across all sixty seven markets when the new program's lottery ran. Of the thirty four markets that had stayed mandatory, twelve landed on the new program's list, thirty five percent. Of the thirty three markets that had dropped to voluntary, only seven landed on it, twenty one percent. For comparison, the true national baseline, every eligible metro and micro area in the country, not just former CJR markets, was about twenty three and a half percent.

The corrected baseline changes the shape of the finding. Markets that went through the old program at all didn't get a discount on the new one's lottery, if anything they did slightly better than the national rate, twenty eight percent against a twenty three and a half percent baseline. And the split inside that group runs in one direction: the markets Medicare once forced to stay mandatory were selected at thirty five percent, well above the national rate, while the ones it let go voluntary landed close to it, at twenty one percent. San Francisco isn't an outlier inside this data, it's sitting in the voluntary tier group, which performed closest to what a blind national draw would predict. Los Angeles, in the higher exposure mandatory tier, is the one whose individual outcome ran against its own group's trend: that tier did better than average overall, and Los Angeles was still in the majority of it that didn't land on the new program's roster.

It's worth being direct about what this doesn't prove. This was one random draw, and a result like thirty five percent versus twenty one percent could reflect something real about how these markets are different, or it could just be the ordinary noise you get from a draw this size. Nobody has run a statistical test to tell those two possibilities apart, and this analysis doesn't claim to have settled it. What can be said cleanly is narrower: in this one draw, having once been forced into the older program tracked with more exposure to the new one, not less, and that's the opposite of what a simple story about unlucky markets losing twice would predict.

There's one more honest caveat. The list of which thirty four markets stayed mandatory is a reconstruction, built to match Medicare's own stated rule, not a copy of an official published table, because Medicare no longer publishes one. It passed every check available before being used here, but it's still a rebuild, and that should stay attached to any claim drawn from it.

Put it together, and the shape of it is this. Los Angeles paying its dues under the old program for nine years bought it nothing in the new one. San Francisco getting released early didn't protect it either, and by one specific measure, the markets that had been forced to stay the longest actually did somewhat better the second time around. Coincidence and pattern turn out to be the same story here, just running backwards from how it first looks.

Modesto and San Francisco Had the Same CJR History. One Got Thirty-Seven TEAM Hospitals. The Other Got Zero.

San Francisco and Modesto both lost mandatory CJR status in twenty eighteen and kept running the program voluntarily through its final day, the same trajectory, right up to TEAM's own separate lottery. San Francisco drew thirty-seven hospitals, mandatorily. Modesto drew none, not even a single voluntary joiner.

Los Angeles has one hospital in Medicare's TEAM model, and it's there because it volunteered. San Francisco has thirty-seven, the most of any market in California, and every one of them is mandatory. Modesto has zero, not mandatory, not voluntary, none.

Los Angeles' history is different from the other two. It was one of only thirty-four markets CMS kept mandatory in an older joint replacement bundle, CJR, when the program narrowed its list in twenty eighteen, staying in for all nine years of CJR's run, twenty sixteen through twenty twenty-four. San Francisco and Modesto share a different history: both were downgraded out of mandatory status in that same twenty eighteen cut, and both kept running CJR voluntarily through the program's final day. Same drop, same continuation, the identical nine years in some form. One of them is now the largest TEAM market in California. The other has nothing.

What TEAM asks of a hospital.

TEAM, the Transforming Episode Accountability Model, is a five year Medicare program that makes a hospital financially responsible for the cost and outcomes of a patient's care for thirty days after five kinds of surgery: lower extremity joint replacement, spinal fusion, coronary artery bypass grafting, surgical hip and femur fracture treatment, and major bowel procedures. Seven hundred twenty hospitals are on the mandatory roster nationwide, selected not by application but by geography: any hospital paid under the inpatient prospective payment system, with a Medicare certification address inside one of a hundred eighty-eight metropolitan or micropolitan areas CMS drew by stratified random sample, out of roughly eight hundred three eligible areas, is in. Outside those hundred eighty-eight areas, a narrow voluntary pathway lets a small number of additional hospitals opt in anyway, if they ran a mandatory bundle of their own, CJR through December thirty-first, twenty twenty-four, or BPCI Advanced through December thirty-first, twenty twenty-five. Ten hospitals did, nationwide.

Three California markets ran CJR for nine years, in different mixes of mandatory and voluntary status. Their outcomes on TEAM's separate, later lottery could not be more different.

Three markets, one history, three outcomes.

California had three metro areas in CJR's original twenty sixteen mandatory lottery: Los Angeles, San Francisco, and Modesto. All three ran under mandatory CJR from twenty sixteen through twenty eighteen. In twenty eighteen, CMS cut mandatory CJR participation to the thirty-four markets with the highest historical episode spending. Los Angeles' spending was high enough to stay in, while San Francisco and Modesto dropped to CJR's voluntary continuation tier. All three, including the two that dropped tiers, kept running CJR episodes through the model's final day, December thirty-first, twenty twenty-four. A hospital only needed to have run the model at some point through that date to qualify later for TEAM's voluntary opt in, and CJR's own hospital list spreadsheets show hospitals in all three markets doing exactly that.

Then TEAM ran its own, unrelated stratified draw in twenty twenty-four, and the three markets split three ways. Los Angeles had stayed mandatory under CJR all nine years, and ended up with one hospital on TEAM, Cedars-Sinai Medical Center, there voluntarily. San Francisco had dropped to CJR's voluntary tier in twenty eighteen, and ended up with thirty-seven hospitals on TEAM, all of them mandatory. Modesto had also dropped to CJR's voluntary tier, and ended up with zero hospitals on TEAM, not selected, and with no voluntary joiner either.

Los Angeles' one hospital is there because it used the voluntary opt in built for hospitals with prior mandatory bundle history. San Francisco's thirty-seven are there because CMS's twenty twenty-four draw happened to select that metro area outright, mandatorily, for reasons unrelated to its CJR history. Modesto's metro area wasn't drawn, and unlike Los Angeles, no hospital inside it used the voluntary pathway to join anyway. Every plausible route onto TEAM's roster came up empty for a market that ran the same nine year CJR program as the other two, under the same voluntary continuation status San Francisco also carried after twenty eighteen.

Confirming the zero.

This finding rests on checking every row of CMS's own roster, not just the mandatory ones. CMS's TEAM participant list carries seven hundred twenty hospitals total, mandatory and voluntary combined. None of them list a CBSA of Modesto, California. A fresh pull of that same file directly from CMS's website, dated a few months later and carrying two more hospitals than the version this article otherwise uses, shows the identical result: zero Modesto rows, and the same one hospital Los Angeles and thirty-seven hospital San Francisco counts. The finding isn't an artifact of a stale file.

It also isn't an artifact of Modesto having no hospitals to send. CMS's own hospital directory lists five acute care hospitals in Stanislaus County, the single county that makes up the Modesto metro area: Doctors Medical Center and Memorial Medical Center in Modesto, Stanislaus Surgical Hospital in Modesto, Emanuel Medical Center in Turlock, and Oak Valley Hospital District in Oakdale. None of the five appears anywhere on TEAM's participant list, mandatory or voluntary.

The voluntary pathway specifically requires prior CJR or BPCI Advanced history through the program's own final performance year. None of the five Stanislaus County hospitals shows up in CMS's list of BPCI Advanced participants for that model's last performance period either, the one running through December thirty-first, twenty twenty-five. So the gap isn't that a Modesto hospital was eligible to volunteer and chose not to. On the record CMS has published, no hospital in Modesto's metro area had a BPCI Advanced pathway available to it at all, and the one pathway that was available, CJR history, went unused.

Why a market with the same history landed differently.

TEAM's selection wasn't blind to CJR history. CMS's own final rule sorted every eligible metro area into a group based on the median of four factors: average historical episode spending, the number of acute care hospitals in the area paid under the inpatient system, the number of safety net hospitals with qualifying episode volume, and, the factor that matters here, past exposure to CMS's own bundled payment models, including CJR and BPCI Advanced, going back to late twenty thirteen. CMS said explicitly, in the rule proposing this design, that it intended to oversample metro areas with low past bundled payment exposure, while still giving some high exposure areas a chance of selection rather than excluding them outright. A market with nine years of CJR participation, mandatory the whole way, like Los Angeles, or mandatory at first and voluntary after twenty eighteen, like San Francisco and Modesto, would plausibly land in the higher exposure group CMS deliberately gave lower draw odds, not zero odds. San Francisco proves the higher exposure group can still be drawn, but at lower odds than a market with no bundled payment history at all.

That mechanism explains the general pattern already documented elsewhere in this research: former mandatory CJR markets nationwide were selected into TEAM at about thirty-five percent, and former voluntary tier markets, San Francisco and Modesto's own group, at about twenty-one percent, both below the roughly forty-nine percent baseline rate for all eligible metro areas. It does not explain why San Francisco drew and Modesto didn't, despite sharing the identical voluntary tier history. That's the ordinary variance of a probabilistic draw, and CMS has not published, and this research did not independently reconstruct, the exact selection probability assigned to Modesto's specific group.

Size is the more concrete difference. Stanislaus County's five acute care hospitals are a small fraction of the hospital count in Los Angeles or San Francisco's much larger metro areas, and the number of eligible hospitals in a metro area is itself one of CMS's four grouping factors. Modesto's metro population is about five hundred fifty-seven thousand, roughly a fortieth of Los Angeles' and a tenth of San Francisco's and Oakland's, and smaller than any of the three biggest one hospital TEAM markets this research has already covered elsewhere, Los Angeles, Miami, and Tampa, whose metro Medicare enrollment runs from roughly seven hundred seventy thousand up to two point three million. A market this size sits far more plausibly in whatever low bucket several of TEAM's four grouping factors define than a market the size of Los Angeles or San Francisco does, a plausible contributor to lower selection odds, though not one this research can isolate cleanly from the past exposure factor above, since CMS classifies metro areas on all four factors jointly rather than one at a time.

It's worth being honest about the limits of this, too. CMS has not published a hospital by hospital reconstruction of exactly which selection probability Modesto's metro area carried, so this piece cannot say precisely how unlikely a zero outcome was, only that it fits a documented, deliberate oversampling design rather than an obvious error. And nothing here should be read as CMS being unaware of, or having erred regarding, Modesto's absence from TEAM. A stratified random draw is expected to produce outcomes like this one for some markets, by design.

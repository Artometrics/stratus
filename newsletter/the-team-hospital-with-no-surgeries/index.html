Narration script

Laguna Honda Hospital & Rehabilitation Center is California’s largest public nursing home. It has no emergency department. It does not perform hip replacements, knee replacements, spinal fusions, heart bypass surgery, or major bowel surgery — the five procedures that CMS’s newest mandatory payment program, the Transforming Episode Accountability Model (TEAM), exists to manage. It spent more than two years with no Medicare or Medicaid certification at all, after federal regulators shut off its funding in 2022 over safety violations.

It is also, as of January 1, 2026, a mandatory TEAM participant, on the hook for the cost and quality of surgeries it does not do.

That isn’t a data error on anyone’s part. It’s what happens when a fifty-year-old hospital license and a geography-based selection rule interact without anyone checking what the hospital behind the license actually does.

What TEAM asks of a hospital

TEAM is a five-year Medicare program that makes a hospital financially responsible for the cost and outcomes of a patient’s care for 30 days after five kinds of surgery: lower-extremity joint replacement, spinal fusion, coronary artery bypass grafting, surgical hip and femur fracture treatment, and major bowel procedures. 720 hospitals are on the roster, selected not by application but by CMS’s own rule: any hospital paid under the Inpatient Prospective Payment System (IPPS), with a CMS Certification Number (CCN) address inside one of 188 metropolitan or micropolitan areas CMS drew by stratified random sample, is mandatory. There is no volume screen in that rule and no review of what a given hospital actually treats. If the hospital’s CCN sits in the right geography and the right regulatory category, it’s in.

Laguna Honda Hospital & Rehabilitation Center (CCN 050668) sits inside the San Francisco-Oakland-Fremont CBSA, one of the mandatory areas, alongside 36 other hospitals. That’s the entire mechanism. Nothing about its patient population or its surgical volume enters into it.

A hospital that does almost none of what the model assumes

CMS’s own public data shows why that matters here specifically. In the Hospital General Information file CMS publishes for Care Compare, Laguna Honda Hospital & Rehabilitation Center is classified as an “Acute Care Hospital” and separately marked as offering no emergency services. In CMS’s Complications and Deaths dataset, eight distinct postoperative-complication measures tied to surgical inpatients — including postoperative hemorrhage, postoperative sepsis, postoperative respiratory failure, and wound dehiscence — are reported for Laguna Honda Hospital & Rehabilitation Center with the footnote “no cases met the criteria for this measure.” Its hip/knee replacement complication rate, and its 30-day mortality rates for CABG, heart attack, and stroke, carry no published rate at all. Its emergency-department volume measure is unreported for the same underlying reason: there isn’t an emergency department generating one.

UCSF’s Orthopaedic Surgery department, which staffs Laguna Honda Hospital & Rehabilitation Center, lists exactly three specialties there: physical medicine and rehabilitation, podiatry, and orthotics and prosthetics. No orthopedic surgeons. The hospital does still admit real, medically complex patients — CMS’s hybrid mortality measure shows 28 qualifying stays in its most recent period, and it reports real caseloads for sepsis-care and pneumonia measures — so this isn’t a hospital with no patients. It’s a hospital whose patients are not having the kind of surgery TEAM was built to track.

Why a nursing-home-style hospital carries a surgical-hospital’s CCN

The explanation is bureaucratic, not clinical. CMS assigns CCNs by facility type in fixed numeric blocks, published in its own cost-report documentation: 0001-0879 for short-term general and specialty hospitals, 2000-2299 for long-term care hospitals (excluded from IPPS), 3025-3099 for rehabilitation hospitals (also excluded from IPPS). Laguna Honda Hospital & Rehabilitation Center’s CCN, 050668, falls inside the short-term general hospital block — the same block as every acute surgical hospital in the country — rather than in either of the two blocks reserved for facilities that function the way it actually does today.

That placement isn’t a clerical mistake CMS is unaware of; it’s the hospital’s actual, current Medicare enrollment category, and TEAM’s eligibility rule reads that category literally. A hospital paid under IPPS in a mandatory CBSA is in, full stop. Nothing in the rule asks whether a hospital with that license is still using it for surgery.

A hospital that had no certification at all for two years

The mismatch is sharper because of what happened in between. In April 2022, CMS terminated Laguna Honda’s Medicare and Medicaid participation entirely, after inspections triggered by two self-reported, non-fatal overdoses on-site turned up broader safety violations — problems with medication handling, infection control, and site security. Federal officials gave the hospital months to close and transfer out its residents. The transfers themselves became controversial: the San Francisco Chronicle reported at least four resident deaths within days to weeks of transfer in mid-2022, and the California Department of Public Health issued a round of citations in December 2022 tied to a larger set of resident relocations, some of which it found were followed by death. Laguna Honda regained Medicaid certification in August 2023 and full Medicare certification in June 2024 — a gap of just over two years with no federal certification of any kind.

None of that history bears on surgical volume. It’s cited here because it underscores what kind of institution this is: a safety-net nursing and rehabilitation hospital, serving San Francisco’s most medically and economically vulnerable residents (Medi-Cal covers about 95% of its funding), that spent much of TEAM’s own run-up fighting to keep its doors open for reasons entirely unrelated to orthopedic or cardiac surgery.

What being on the list actually costs it

TEAM’s own design limits the practical damage here. The model includes a low-volume policy: a participant with 30 or fewer episodes in a given surgical category over the three-year baseline period faces no downside financial risk in that category for the corresponding performance year, though it can still receive a reconciliation payment if it happens to come in under target. Given that CMS’s own quality data shows zero qualifying postoperative-surgical cases across most of the categories TEAM tracks, Laguna Honda Hospital & Rehabilitation Center likely has at or near zero episodes in every one of the five bundles — which means it is formally a mandatory participant with, in practice, nothing to reconcile. CMS has also said TEAM adds no separate reporting burden beyond a hospital’s existing quality-reporting programs, so the compliance cost of being on the list, for a hospital that runs no relevant episodes, is close to nothing.

That’s the actual shape of the story: not a hospital being squeezed by a payment model it can’t handle, but a hospital carried on a mandatory list built for a kind of care it stopped meaningfully providing, kept there by a licensing category nobody revisited when the selection rule was written.

What isn’t established here

CMS’s quality-measure footnotes (“no cases met the criteria for this measure”) are strong evidence of zero or near-zero surgical volume in the categories they cover, but they are not the same as a CMS statement of Laguna Honda’s actual TEAM episode count, which CMS has not published. Whether Laguna Honda is one of a kind or one of several former-acute-care CCNs swept into TEAM the same way is untested — it would take a full facility-type check against every one of the 720 hospitals on the roster, which this piece did not do. And nothing here says CMS is unaware of the mismatch; it may simply consider a near-zero-impact mandatory listing not worth an exception process.

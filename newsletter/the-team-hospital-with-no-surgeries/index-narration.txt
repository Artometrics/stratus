Why Is a Nursing Home With No Operating Room on Medicare's Mandatory Surgery List?

Laguna Honda Hospital has no emergency room and, by CMS's own quality data, performs none of the five surgeries Medicare's TEAM model is built around. It's on the mandatory roster anyway, for a reason that has nothing to do with what the hospital does.

Laguna Honda Hospital and Rehabilitation Center is California's largest public nursing home. It has no emergency department. It does not perform hip replacements, knee replacements, spinal fusions, heart bypass surgery, or major bowel surgery, the five procedures that CMS's newest mandatory payment program, the Transforming Episode Accountability Model, exists to manage. It spent more than two years with no Medicare or Medicaid certification at all, after federal regulators shut off its funding in twenty twenty-two over safety violations.

It is also, as of January first, twenty twenty-six, a mandatory TEAM participant, on the hook for the cost and quality of surgeries it does not do.

That isn't a data error on anyone's part. It's what happens when a fifty-year-old hospital license and a geography-based selection rule interact without anyone checking what the hospital behind the license actually does.

What TEAM asks of a hospital.

TEAM is a five-year Medicare program that makes a hospital financially responsible for the cost and outcomes of a patient's care for thirty days after five kinds of surgery: lower-extremity joint replacement, spinal fusion, coronary artery bypass grafting, surgical hip and femur fracture treatment, and major bowel procedures. Seven hundred twenty hospitals are on the roster, selected not by application but by CMS's own rule: any hospital paid under the inpatient prospective payment system, with a Medicare certification address inside one of a hundred eighty-eight metropolitan or micropolitan areas CMS drew by stratified random sample, is mandatory. There is no volume screen in that rule and no review of what a given hospital actually treats. If the hospital's certification sits in the right geography and the right regulatory category, it's in.

Laguna Honda sits inside the San Francisco, Oakland, Fremont metro area, one of the mandatory areas, alongside thirty-six other hospitals. That's the entire mechanism. Nothing about its patient population or its surgical volume enters into it.

A hospital that does almost none of what the model assumes.

CMS's own public data shows why that matters here specifically. In the general information file CMS publishes for its Care Compare website, Laguna Honda is classified as an acute care hospital and separately marked as offering no emergency services. In CMS's own complications and deaths data, eight distinct postoperative complication measures tied to surgical inpatients, including postoperative hemorrhage, postoperative sepsis, postoperative respiratory failure, and wound dehiscence, are reported for Laguna Honda with the note that no cases met the criteria for the measure. Its hip and knee replacement complication rate, and its thirty-day mortality rates for bypass surgery, heart attack, and stroke, carry no published rate at all. Its emergency department volume measure is unreported for the same underlying reason: there isn't an emergency department generating one.

U.C.S.F.'s orthopaedic surgery department, which staffs Laguna Honda, lists exactly three specialties there: physical medicine and rehabilitation, podiatry, and orthotics and prosthetics. No orthopedic surgeons. The hospital does still admit real, medically complex patients, CMS's own hospital-wide mortality measure shows twenty-eight qualifying stays in its most recent reporting period, and it reports real caseloads for sepsis care and pneumonia measures, so this isn't a hospital with no patients. It's a hospital whose patients are not having the kind of surgery TEAM was built to track.

Why a nursing-home-style hospital carries a surgical hospital's license.

The explanation is bureaucratic, not clinical. CMS sorts every hospital's Medicare certification number into blocks by facility type, in ranges it publishes in its own cost report documentation. The first block, running from the very start of the numbering system up through the upper eight hundreds, is for short-term general and specialty hospitals. A separate block in the two thousands is for long-term care hospitals, which are excluded from this payment system entirely. Another block just above three thousand is for rehabilitation hospitals, also excluded. Laguna Honda's certification number falls inside that very first block, the same block as every acute surgical hospital in the country, rather than in either of the two blocks reserved for facilities that function the way it actually does today.

That placement isn't a clerical mistake CMS is unaware of. It's the hospital's actual, current Medicare enrollment category, and TEAM's eligibility rule reads that category literally. A hospital paid under the inpatient system in a mandatory metro area is in, full stop. Nothing in the rule asks whether a hospital with that license is still using it for surgery.

A hospital that had no certification at all for two years.

The mismatch is sharper because of what happened in between. In April of twenty twenty-two, CMS terminated Laguna Honda's Medicare and Medicaid participation entirely, after inspections triggered by two self-reported, non-fatal overdoses on site turned up broader safety violations, problems with medication handling, infection control, and site security. Federal officials gave the hospital months to close and transfer out its residents. The transfers themselves became controversial: the San Francisco Chronicle reported at least four resident deaths within days to weeks of transfer in mid twenty twenty-two, and the California Department of Public Health issued a round of citations in December of that year tied to a larger set of resident relocations, some of which it found were followed by death. Laguna Honda regained Medicaid certification in August of twenty twenty-three and full Medicare certification in June of twenty twenty-four, a gap of just over two years with no federal certification of any kind.

None of that history bears on surgical volume. It's worth knowing because it underscores what kind of institution this is: a safety net nursing and rehabilitation hospital, serving San Francisco's most medically and economically vulnerable residents, Medicaid covers about ninety-five percent of its funding, that spent much of TEAM's own run-up fighting to keep its doors open for reasons entirely unrelated to orthopedic or cardiac surgery.

What being on the list actually costs it.

TEAM's own design limits the practical damage here. The model includes a low-volume policy: a participant with thirty or fewer episodes in a given surgical category over the three-year baseline period faces no downside financial risk in that category for the matching performance year, though it can still receive a payment if it happens to come in under target. Given that CMS's own quality data shows zero qualifying postoperative surgical cases across most of the categories TEAM tracks, Laguna Honda likely has at or near zero episodes in every one of the five bundles, which means it is formally a mandatory participant with, in practice, nothing to reconcile. CMS has also said TEAM adds no separate reporting burden beyond a hospital's existing quality reporting programs, so the compliance cost of being on the list, for a hospital that runs no relevant episodes, is close to nothing.

That's the actual shape of the story: not a hospital being squeezed by a payment model it can't handle, but a hospital carried on a mandatory list built for a kind of care it stopped meaningfully providing, kept there by a licensing category nobody revisited when the selection rule was written.

It's worth being honest about the limits of this, too. CMS's own footnote, that no cases met the criteria for a given measure, is strong evidence of zero or near-zero surgical volume in the categories it covers, but it isn't the same as CMS actually publishing Laguna Honda's TEAM episode count, which it hasn't done. Whether Laguna Honda is one of a kind, or one of several former acute-care hospitals swept into TEAM the same way, is untested; that would take checking every single hospital on the seven hundred twenty hospital roster against its actual facility type, which this piece did not do. And nothing here suggests CMS is unaware of the mismatch. It may simply consider a near-zero-impact mandatory listing not worth building an exception process around.

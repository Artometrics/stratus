Download eBook
TL;DR
  • Health data carries dual obligations.
  • Late lawyers cause project delays.
  • Trust—not technology—blocks care interoperability.
  • Thin margins punish bad procurement.
  • AI evaluation requires clinical governance.
  • Technology must redefine continuous care.

Based on Episode 11 of the Rainfall Health Podcast: Cora Han, Chief Health Data Officer at the University of California Health (UC Health) and former senior enforcement attorney in the Federal Trade Commission’s Division of Privacy and Identity Protection, in conversation with Rainfall Health founder and CEO Ahmed “Eddie” Qureshi.

Most conversations about health data start with the pipe: the API, the FHIR standard, the electronic health record integration, or the vendor portal.

Cora Han’s starts on the opposite side of the table: with trust, institutional obligation, and legal sequencing.

As Chief Health Data Officer at University of California Health — overseeing health data governance across an academic health system comprising five medical centers, dozens of clinics, and thousands of care teams — Han guides how patient data is protected, managed, and put to work at scale.

Her vantage point is virtually unique in American healthcare. She trained as an intellectual property attorney handling copyright, trademark, and privacy litigation before spending nearly a decade at the Federal Trade Commission’s (FTC) Division of Privacy and Identity Protection. There, she led high-stakes technology enforcement actions alongside regulatory partners at the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), the Office of the National Coordinator for Health IT (ONC), the Office for Civil Rights (OCR), and the FDA.

At the FTC, she saw what happens when sensitive consumer and patient data is mishandled, monetized without consent, or left unprotected. At UC Health, she sees the opposite: how much clinical good that exact same data unlocks when mobilized responsibly across a care continuum.

It is really our duty to be able to use health data to improve care, to be able to improve people’s lives.

— Cora Han

That duality means Han reads neither as an organizational brake nor as an uncritical technology accelerator. For health system executives preparing for mandatory bundled models like CMS TEAM, CIOs evaluating generative AI, and digital health founders building for health systems, her counsel is fundamentally about sequencing: aligning the clinical workflow, stakeholder incentives, and governance guardrails long before signing software contracts.

What Does a Chief Health Data Officer Actually Do?

To outside observers, a data governance role sounds like an endless cycle of policy memos and regulatory compliance checklists. In practice, Han describes the role as fundamentally operational.

Every morning begins with a concrete problem that someone wants health data to solve:

  1. Operations: Enabling hospital operations teams to track bed availability, surgical throughput, and staffing ratios across five distinct medical campuses.
  2. Population Health: Helping clinical quality leaders identify patients with chronic diseases who are due for preventive screenings before acute complications develop.
  3. Efficiency: Streamlining administrative workflows so physicians and nurses spend less time charting and more time with patients.
  4. Translating Research to Bedside: Creating secure, compliant data enclaves where academic researchers and clinicians can collaborate to develop new therapeutics, validate algorithms, and test novel care pathways.

In every case, the technical capability to move the data is rarely the hardest part. The challenge is ensuring that data use serves a verified institutional need, complies with federal and state privacy statutes, and preserves patient confidence.

The Two Sides of Health Data: Obligation vs. Guardrail

In consumer software, data optimization drives clicks and ad revenue. In healthcare, the stakes are clinical outcomes and human lives. Withholding data or trapping it in clinical silos directly compromises care quality, causes duplicate diagnostic testing, delays post-acute placement, and drives up preventable readmissions.

When health system leaders treat privacy purely as a justification to say “no,” they neglect their clinical duty to use data to improve care. Conversely, when technology vendors view clinical records as training fodder for commercial foundation models, they risk devastating regulatory penalties and institutional reputational damage.

Why Do Lawyers Get Blamed for Slowing Down Innovation?

One of the most persistent refrains among health technology founders and clinical innovators is that legal and compliance teams are “where good ideas go to die.”

Han rejects that framing outright:

Lawyers don’t slow projects down. Late lawyers do.

— Cora Han

The breakdown occurs when a clinical or operational team spends nine to twelve months evaluating a software vendor, refining a pilot scope, and configuring dashboards, only to hand the completed vendor agreement to legal two weeks before the targeted go-live date.

At that late stage, in-house counsel uncovers fundamental structural flaws:

  • Overbroad data rights that run afoul of HIPAA or state privacy statutes (such as California’s CMIA).
  • Inadequate cybersecurity protocols or undisclosed downstream sub-processors.
  • Ambiguous data retention, re-identification, or secondary model-training clauses.
  • Workflow assumptions that create regulatory or clinical liability under Medicare programs.

When legal raises these critical flags, the project grinds to a halt. The operational champions blame legal for obstruction. In reality, the initiative stalled because governance was treated as an afterthought rather than a primary architecture requirement.

How to Build Multidisciplinary Alignment

To avoid the late-lawyer trap, health systems must establish a structured, multidisciplinary table from day one:

  • Dedicated Governance Forums: Convening clinical champions, IT security engineers, and legal counsel on a recurring schedule so evaluations do not stall in scheduling purgatory.
  • A Shared Vocabulary: Clinicians, data scientists, and attorneys must learn enough of each other’s domain to communicate without suspicion. Compliance concerns must be specific and grounded, not used as a generic veto.
  • A Single Problem Statement: Defining precisely what clinical friction is being resolved, why existing systems cannot solve it, and what minimal data set is required.

Why Does Real-World Interoperability Break Down Between Hospitals and SNFs?

Under CMS’s mandatory Transforming Episode Accountability Model (CMS TEAM), 721 hospitals are held financially accountable for total Medicare Part A and B spending across a 30-day post-discharge window following five major inpatient surgical episodes:

  • Lower-extremity joint replacement (LEJR)
  • Surgical hip and femur fracture treatment (SHFFT)
  • Coronary artery bypass graft (CABG)
  • Major bowel procedures
  • Spinal fusion

Because the majority of complications, readmissions, and spending occur after the patient leaves the acute care hospital — in skilled nursing facilities (SNFs), inpatient rehabilitation facilities, and home health agencies — TEAM is, in practice, a distributed care coordination and data-sharing mandate.

When asked why data does not flow smoothly across this continuum, Han chooses a precise word: uneven.

Care Continuum Setting Technology Infrastructure Dedicated Resources Primary Operational Focus
Acute Care Hospitals Enterprise EHR (Epic/Cerner), real-time HL7/FHIR feeds, automated clinical alert systems In-house legal counsel, data security engineers, clinical informaticists Acute stabilization, surgical intervention, throughput, bed capacity
Skilled Nursing (SNFs) Fragmented point-of-care EHRs, manual portal data entry, legacy software Lean administrative teams, high nursing turnover, outsourced IT Functional rehabilitation, ADL recovery, medication administration
Community Organizations Paper charts, spreadsheets, unscheduled phone calls, secure faxes Social workers, patient navigators, family caregivers, case managers Social drivers of health (SDOH), housing, nutrition, transportation

The friction across this care continuum does not stem from a lack of goodwill. Surgeons, hospital case managers, SNF directors of nursing, and home health coordinators all want the patient to heal without complication. The breakdown stems from four structural disparities:

  1. Storage Disparities: Legacy EHR platforms that do not speak the same clinical dialects or format transition summaries consistently.
  2. Process Fragmentation: Incompatible handoff timelines, referral documentation standards, and medication reconciliation workflows.
  3. Resource Asymmetry: Large medical systems possess multi-million-dollar health informatics teams; post-acute facilities often rely on single-person contracted IT support.
  4. Data Capture Variance: The acute-care chart captures surgical minutiae; the post-acute facility needs functional mobility, wound status, and social support availability.

As Eddie Qureshi emphasized during the discussion, health policy cannot assume an idealized world where every nursing home runs the same infrastructure as an academic medical center. Technology must meet providers where they operate today.

It’s Not the Pipes. It’s the Trust.

For more than two decades, the healthcare industry has treated interoperability as a plumbing problem: deploy FHIR APIs, connect to regional health information exchanges (HIEs), establish Direct secure messaging protocols, and data will flow.

Yet data sharing across acute and post-acute care remains persistently fragmented. Why?

Asked for the biggest challenge, she offers one broad word: There is a trust gap. It’s not the pipes. It’s the trust.

— Cora Han

Han dissects this trust gap across two operational planes:

1. Trust Between Institutional Care Partners

Hospitals and post-acute facilities are frequently competitors in certain outpatient service lines while being forced collaborators under bundled payment models. When a hospital requests direct access to a post-acute partner’s clinical records, immediate questions arise:

  • Will you use our clinical documentation to evaluate, penalize, or deselect our facility from your preferred network?
  • How will the data be transmitted, and who assumes financial liability if a breach occurs?
  • Who is legally liable if a missed notification or incomplete transfer leads to an adverse patient event?

Without bounded, pre-negotiated legal frameworks, providers default to defensive data withholding.

2. Trust With Patients

Patients are acutely aware of the commercialization of personal information. If patients fear their surgical details, biometric readings, or health status will be shared with data brokers, commercial insurers, or advertisers, they withhold sensitive details from their doctors.

True interoperability unlocks only when both planes are resolved: institutions agree on purpose-bound operational scopes, and patients know their information is guarded by enforceable institutional commitments.

What Makes a Health Tech Vendor a True Partner?

For technology companies seeking to partner with health systems, Han shares three core principles that accelerate enterprise adoption far more effectively than a slide deck of software features:

1. Respect the Existing Clinical Workflow

Health systems do not have the operational capacity or clinician goodwill to mandate yet another standalone login portal. Technology that forces doctors or care managers to toggle between windows will be ignored. Software must integrate natively into established daily workflows.

2. Explain Plainly How the Technology Works

Proprietary “black-box” algorithms will not pass clinical governance committees. Vendors must be capable of demonstrating to medical directors and ethics boards why a model generates specific recommendations, what validation datasets were used, and how algorithmic bias was measured and mitigated.

3. Answer Security Questionnaires Fast and Fully

Nothing stalls health system procurement faster than a half-filled security questionnaire or vague answers regarding sub-processors. Vendors that arrive with standardized, pre-packaged SOC2 Type II reports, penetration testing summaries, and architectural diagrams build immediate trust.

A Car Is Not a Fast Horse: Why AI Will Redefine Care Delivery

As the conversation concluded, Eddie Qureshi and Cora Han turned to the long-term trajectory of artificial intelligence across American healthcare.

When the automobile first appeared, early observers described it as a “horseless carriage.” The metric of success was straightforward: does it pull a carriage slightly faster or cheaper than a horse? Only years later did society recognize that automobiles fundamentally transformed urban planning, commerce, supply chains, and public health.

Healthcare is currently navigating its “fast horse” era of AI. Systems are deploying tools to draft patient portal replies slightly faster, summarize visit notes a few minutes quicker, or automate routine scheduling calls.

Don’t stop at making existing things go faster. Ask how technology might change the way care is provided. It is really kind of our duty to think in the big picture about what the large-scale potential could be.

— Cora Han

When true care-continuum infrastructure is established — connecting acute hospitals, post-acute facilities, and community health workers through shared governance and accountability — healthcare will stop trying to make fragmented, analog processes run faster. It will finally deliver the continuous, proactive care transitions that patients deserve.

Frequently asked questions

What does a Chief Health Data Officer do at a major health system?

A Chief Health Data Officer (CHDO) oversees health data governance, security, and utilization strategy. At institutions like UC Health, the CHDO bridges clinical operations, IT engineering, research data enclaves, and legal compliance to ensure patient data is used effectively to improve clinical outcomes while safeguarding privacy and institutional trust.

Why do care transitions fail between acute hospitals and skilled nursing facilities (SNFs)?

Care transitions between hospitals and SNFs typically break down due to structural disparities in health IT infrastructure, incompatible workflow handoffs, and a lack of pre-negotiated trust agreements. While both parties want the patient to heal, defensive data withholding and fragmented EHR systems prevent clinical teams from accessing actionable patient information in real time.

How does health data governance affect compliance under the CMS TEAM model?

Under CMS TEAM, hospitals are held financially accountable for all Medicare Part A and B spending across a 30-day post-discharge window. Because post-acute providers deliver the majority of care during this period, hospitals must establish secure, compliant, and purpose-bound data agreements to track patient progress, prevent complications, and avoid readmission penalties without violating HIPAA.

Why do health system lawyers get a reputation for slowing down technology adoption?

As Cora Han explains, “Lawyers don’t slow projects down. Late lawyers do.” When operational or clinical teams negotiate software contracts for months before bringing in legal counsel, lawyers are forced to halt deployments to resolve critical privacy, cybersecurity, and secondary data-use vulnerabilities that should have been addressed during initial scoping.

What should health system CIOs ask before procuring healthcare AI software?

CIOs and clinical executives should evaluate eight fundamental governance domains: clinical necessity, comparative clinical efficacy, data minimization, data retention policies, secondary model-training restrictions, model drift notification protocols, ongoing algorithmic auditing, and enterprise cybersecurity certifications (SOC2 Type II, HITRUST).

Hospital Readmissions and CMS TEAM: 48% of Hospital-Condition Pairs Are Still Failing the 30-Day Standard

An analysis of 11,720 hospital-condition pairs under HRRP reveals the readmissions problem that CMS TEAM was built to address — and which states and procedures carry the highest risk.

Jennifer Tim-Diamond
· July 22, 2026
TL;DR
  • HRRP still penalizes readmissions
  • TEAM adds full episode cost risk
  • Overlap raises financial exposure
  • Care transitions need redesign

The Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program has been running since 2012. The premise is simple: if your patients return to the hospital within 30 days of discharge at a higher rate than CMS’s risk model expects given their medical profile, CMS docks your Medicare reimbursements. The penalty caps at 3% of all Medicare payments — not just payments for the penalized condition.

More than a decade later, the data says the program has defined the problem without solving it.

Fast facts

  • 48.1% of HRRP-eligible hospital-condition pairs exceed the 1.0 ERR threshold — more readmissions than CMS models predict.
  • 6 conditions tracked: AMI, Heart Failure, Pneumonia, COPD, Hip/Knee Arthroplasty (LEJR), and CABG.
  • 1.00178 average excess readmission ratio nationally — the average hospital readmits slightly more patients than CMS models expect.
  • Hip/Knee has the highest average ERR at 1.00399 — the most difficult readmission problem to control under HRRP.
  • New Jersey leads all states at 65.4% of hospital-condition pairs penalized — the highest-risk geography in the dataset.
  • 721 hospitals are now mandated under CMS TEAM, where readmission performance within the 30-day episode directly affects reimbursement reconciliation.

The geography of the penalty

New Jersey leads all states at 65.4% — nearly two out of every three hospital-condition pairs performing worse than CMS models predict. Massachusetts (62.5%) and Mississippi (61.1%) follow. New Jersey hosts some of the country’s highest-revenue hospital systems — Hackensack Meridian Health, RWJBarnabas — while Mississippi is rural and chronically underfunded. The same penalty outcome across fundamentally different systems.

Illinois (57.8%), Florida (57.5%), and Georgia (55.8%) cluster in the high-penalty tier. California (55.2%) and New York (52.7%) — both high-TEAM-concentration states — are also above the national average. This is not a rural poverty problem with a clean geographic boundary. High-resource markets are not immune.

The chart shows states above the national average — but even the better-performing states are not clean. Below-average means a smaller share of hospital-condition pairs exceed ERR 1.0, not zero penalties.

Hip/knee is the unresolved condition

All six HRRP conditions carry an average ERR above 1.0. But Hip/Knee (LEJR) stands apart. Its average ERR of 1.00399 is more than twice the gap of the next closest condition (CABG at 1.00179). That difference may look small in absolute terms. Multiplied across approximately 1.3 million joint replacements performed annually in the U.S., it represents a large volume of preventable returns.

The mechanism is structural. Elective joint replacement surgery has shortened its inpatient stay significantly over the past decade. What was a three-to-four-day admission is now often an outpatient or same-day procedure. That shift moves the readmission risk window entirely into post-discharge days — days hospitals cannot monitor in real time without deliberate post-acute coordination infrastructure.

This is directly relevant to CMS TEAM. LEJR is the highest-volume TEAM procedure at 58% of all episodes. Under TEAM, a readmission within 30 days of discharge is not just an HRRP penalty event — it is an episode cost that falls entirely on the hospital’s TEAM reconciliation account. The same failure that generates an HRRP penalty will also likely push the TEAM episode above its target price.

HRRP defined the problem; TEAM raises the stakes

HRRP has been in effect since 2012. The fact that 48.1% of hospital-condition pairs still exceed the 1.0 ERR threshold more than a decade later says something about the limits of per-readmission financial penalties as a behavior change mechanism. Hospitals have responded — readmission rates have declined since HRRP launched — but the program has not solved the underlying problem.

CMS TEAM, mandatory for 721 hospitals starting January 1, 2026, goes further. TEAM attaches the entire 30-day episode cost to reimbursement — not just the readmission event. A hospital that manages to avoid the readmission but runs up high SNF, home health, or outpatient costs in the post-discharge window can still lose money on the episode. HRRP is where the penalty starts. TEAM is where the stakes get real.

The hospitals that have struggled under HRRP — high-readmission hospitals in New Jersey, Massachusetts, Illinois, and Florida — are the same hospitals most likely to be in scope for TEAM. The data in this analysis describes the problem TEAM was designed to address.

Data methodology: how the HRRP ERR is computed

The excess readmission ratio (ERR) is a hierarchical logistic regression model estimate produced annually by CMS for each hospital-condition pair. The model controls for patient age, sex, principal diagnosis, comorbidities, and prior utilization using Medicare Part A claims. An ERR of 1.0 means the hospital readmitted exactly as many patients as its risk-adjusted peer group would predict. Above 1.0 means excess readmissions; below 1.0 means fewer readmissions than predicted.

The FY2024 HRRP dataset analyzed here (CMS Provider Data Catalog, Dataset 9n3s-kdb3) covers the measurement period July 1, 2021 through June 30, 2024 — a three-year rolling window. CMS suppresses hospital-condition pairs where the hospital has fewer than 25 discharges for the condition in the measurement period, to protect statistical reliability. The suppression is systematic: small rural hospitals disproportionately fall below the threshold and disappear from the analysis. The 11,720 valid records analyzed represent a 2,700+ hospital subset weighted toward larger, busier facilities. Every finding in this analysis should be read with that structural bias in mind.

The analysis was produced from the CMS open data API: https://data.cms.gov/provider-data/api/1/datastore/query/9n3s-kdb3/0 — the same endpoint used in the Artometrics READMITTED analysis (artometrics.com/readmitted, April 2026). The 18,330 total records retrieved include both suppressed and non-suppressed rows; ERR computations are based on the 11,720 rows with valid, non-suppressed ERR values.

(Source: Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services. (2024). HRRP FY2025 supplemental data, Dataset 9n3s-kdb3.)

Ownership type, for-profits, and the structural penalty distribution

Analyzing the HRRP dataset by hospital ownership type reveals a consistent pattern: for-profit hospitals carry a modestly higher share of penalized hospital-condition pairs than nonprofit and government-owned hospitals. In the FY2024 data, the for-profit sector runs approximately 8–10 percentage points above the nonprofit sector in the share of pairs exceeding ERR 1.0.

This gap reflects several dynamics. For-profit hospitals — particularly the large investor-owned chains HCA Healthcare, Tenet Health, and Steward Health — have faced sustained shareholder pressure on cost reduction and length-of-stay management that can accelerate discharge at the cost of post-discharge continuity. Shorter hospital stays reduce readmission risk for some patients and increase it for others; the net effect in the HRRP data is a modest excess penalty rate.

Under CMS TEAM, the ownership-type dynamic is complicated by the fact that HCA Healthcare’s BPCI Advanced experience gives its for-profit hospitals episode management infrastructure that many nonprofits lack. HCA’s data analytics capabilities may partially offset the behavioral incentive effects captured in the HRRP data. Tenet Health and Steward Health — the latter having entered bankruptcy in 2024 — present more uncertain pictures: their TEAM episode management infrastructure varies significantly by hospital and market.

(Source: Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services. (2024). Hospital General Information, Dataset xubh-q36u.)

Frequently asked questions

What is the excess readmission ratio (ERR)?

The ERR is a ratio of a hospital’s predicted readmissions to its expected readmissions, where expected is risk-adjusted for the patient population’s age, comorbidities, and discharge history. An ERR above 1.0 means more readmissions than CMS models predict.

What share of hospitals fail the HRRP readmission benchmark?

48.1% of hospital-condition pairs in the FY2024 HRRP dataset exceed the ERR 1.0 threshold, according to analysis of the CMS Provider Data Catalog (dataset 9n3s-kdb3).

Which state has the worst HRRP readmission performance?

New Jersey leads all states at 65.4% of hospital-condition pairs above ERR 1.0, followed by Massachusetts (62.5%) and Mississippi (61.1%).

How does HRRP relate to CMS TEAM?

HRRP penalizes specific readmission events. CMS TEAM goes further by attaching the entire 30-day post-discharge episode cost to reimbursement. A readmission under TEAM is both an HRRP penalty event and an episode cost event — the same failure hits twice.

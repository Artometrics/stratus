CMS TEAM in Massachusetts: Thirty-Seven Mandated Hospitals, Strong on Hip and Knee, Hot on Everything Else.

In brief. Massachusetts carries 37 hospitals in the Transforming Episode Accountability Model, all of them mandatory, spread across four Core-Based Statistical Areas, with 30 of the 37, 81 percent of the state, concentrated in the Boston metro market. The claim that circulates about Massachusetts, that it has a 62.5 percent HRRP penalty rate and the second-worst readmissions profile in the country, is wrong on both counts. The real pooled rate is 57.9 percent against 48.1 percent nationally, worse than average but nowhere near second nationally. And the more useful finding is in how that number breaks down: Massachusetts hospitals actually outperform the country on hip and knee replacement, the one procedure category TEAM prices most heavily. The exposure sits almost entirely in the medical conditions Medicare also tracks under this program, not in the surgical categories TEAM's episode payments are built around.

Section one. At a glance.

Massachusetts has 37 hospitals mandated to participate in the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services' Transforming Episode Accountability Model, known as TEAM, across four Core-Based Statistical Areas. All 37 are mandatory participants; none joined voluntarily. Thirty of them, 81 percent of the state total, sit inside the Boston, Cambridge, Newton, Massachusetts, New Hampshire metropolitan market. The other seven split across three smaller markets: Providence, Warwick, Rhode Island and Massachusetts with 4 hospitals, Barnstable Town on Cape Cod with 2, and Worcester with 1, UMass Memorial Medical Center.

The concentration of academic medical centers in the Boston market has no real parallel elsewhere in American healthcare. Massachusetts General Hospital, Brigham and Women's Hospital, Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, Tufts Medical Center, and Boston Medical Center are all on this list, subject to the exact same TEAM accountability rules as every community hospital in the state.

Section two. Where the mandate sits.

Massachusetts's TEAM footprint is not evenly distributed, it is heavily weighted toward one metro market. Boston, Cambridge, Newton holds 30 of the state's 37 hospitals. The remaining markets are small: four hospitals around Providence, Rhode Island, which spans the Massachusetts border, two on Cape Cod, and one in Worcester. CMS does not publish hospital-level TEAM episode volumes, so this report does not model a Massachusetts-specific episode count. That number would be invented, not measured.

Section three. Readmission performance where TEAM pays.

Now to the part of the analysis that actually predicts how these hospitals are likely to fare under the model: readmissions. This analysis matched Massachusetts's TEAM hospitals to Medicare's public readmissions file using their federal certification numbers, so the linkage is exact. Thirty-six of the 37 have at least one reportable ratio in the current file, covering the period July 2021 through June 2024.

Here's the headline, and it corrects a number that circulates widely. Across every condition Medicare tracks, 57.9 percent of Massachusetts's hospital-condition pairs exceed the excess readmission threshold, against 48.1 percent nationally. That is a real gap, worse than the national average, but it is not the 62.5 percent, second-highest-in-the-country figure attached to Massachusetts elsewhere. Checking every TEAM-participating state directly shows Massachusetts is not in second place. New Jersey, at 61.9 percent across 44 hospitals, and Florida, at 61.4 percent across 40 hospitals, both post higher pooled rates among states with large TEAM rosters. A few states with very small rosters, Indiana with 6 hospitals, Georgia with 13, Missouri with 6, post higher rates still, but a number computed from six hospitals is far noisier than one computed from 37. Put plainly, Massachusetts sits around sixth among large-roster TEAM states by this measure, not second.

The more useful finding is in how that number breaks down by condition. On hip and knee replacement, the procedure category TEAM prices most heavily, Massachusetts actually runs favorably: only 32.4 percent of the state's hip and knee pairs exceed the threshold, against 47.9 percent nationally, well under the national rate. Coronary bypass sits close to parity, 54.5 percent against 49.9 percent nationally. The gap opens up entirely in the medical conditions HRRP also tracks, outside TEAM's surgical categories. Heart attack runs 67.9 percent against 49.7 percent nationally. Heart failure runs 60 percent against 48.9 percent nationally. Pneumonia runs 65.7 percent against 46.8 percent nationally. COPD also runs 65.7 percent against 47.2 percent nationally. Those four medical conditions are what pull the state's overall number above the national line, not the surgical categories TEAM actually pays for.

Massachusetts is not an under-resourced state. Mass General Brigham and Beth Israel Lahey Health are both large, well-capitalized systems with extensive post-acute networks and some of the most sophisticated care-management infrastructure in the country. A couple of things could plausibly explain the medical-condition gap anyway. First, complexity concentration: Massachusetts's academic centers treat a disproportionate share of complex, often out-of-state-referred patients, whose post-discharge needs may exceed what Medicare's risk adjustment fully captures. Second, market density: the Boston metro area has a lot of skilled nursing and home health capacity, but also a lot of competing acute-care systems drawing on that same capacity at once. Neither of these is something this analysis can verify from the data available, they're offered as plausible explanations, not findings.

For TEAM purposes, the practical takeaway is specific, not sweeping. A Massachusetts hospital should not assume its overall HRRP reputation predicts its TEAM performance. On the one procedure category TEAM prices most heavily, hip and knee replacement, the state's mandated hospitals are running ahead of the country, not behind it.

Section four. What this analysis does not estimate.

This report does not put a dollar figure on Massachusetts's TEAM exposure. Medicare doesn't publish hospital-level target prices or episode volumes for TEAM, so any specific dollar number circulating for Massachusetts is somebody's estimate, not a published fact. Optum Advisory projected in 2025 that 56 percent of TEAM hospitals nationally will lose money once downside risk begins, averaging 1.2 million dollars per facility. That's a national number, not a Massachusetts one. Because Massachusetts's readmissions exposure sits mostly in medical conditions TEAM doesn't price rather than in the surgical categories it does, this report doesn't attach a Massachusetts-specific dollar figure to that national estimate. A hospital that wants an actual estimate needs its own CMS-issued target price file.

Section five. Considerations for hospital leadership.

A few things follow directly from the data for a Massachusetts hospital in this program. First, don't assume your overall HRRP reputation predicts your TEAM performance, pull your own hip and knee ratio specifically, since that's the category TEAM actually prices and it's exactly where Massachusetts as a whole runs favorably. Second, if your hospital runs hot on heart attack, heart failure, pneumonia, or COPD readmissions, treat that as a distinct problem from your TEAM exposure, since those conditions sit outside what TEAM's episode payments are built around, even though they still matter for your separate HRRP penalty. Third, if you're a safety-net or smaller community hospital outside the Boston metro core, in Worcester or on Cape Cod, your post-acute network constraints are a different problem than what the large academic systems face, plan accordingly. And fourth, use Performance Year one, the year with no downside risk, to actually test whether the coordination discipline behind your hip and knee performance can be extended to the medical conditions where the state runs hot, because that gap is exactly the kind of problem that shows up as cost once two-sided risk starts in 2027.

Section six. Methods and limitations.

Hospital counts and CBSA assignment come from the CMS TEAM participant list, matched by federal certification number. Readmission figures come from CMS's Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program dataset, joined to the same roster by certification number, covering the July 2021 through June 2024 performance period. This report does not model episode dollar amounts, target prices, or gain and loss projections for Massachusetts specifically, because CMS does not publish the hospital-level data that would make such a model verifiable rather than invented.

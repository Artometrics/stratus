CMS TEAM in New Jersey: Forty-Four Hospitals and the Nation's Worst Readmissions Profile Among Large TEAM States.

In brief. New Jersey carries 44 hospitals in the Transforming Episode Accountability Model, all of them inside a single metropolitan market: New York, Newark, and Jersey City, shared with New York's 65 mandated hospitals. Unlike New York, which turns out to look fine on closer inspection, New Jersey's reputation for bad readmissions holds up. Across every condition Medicare tracks, 62 percent of New Jersey's hospital-condition pairs exceed the excess readmission threshold, against 48 percent nationally. That is the worst readmissions profile of any state with a large TEAM roster this analysis checked, and unlike New York, New Jersey doesn't get to point to a favorable surgical exception. On hip and knee replacement specifically, the procedure TEAM weights most heavily, New Jersey runs worse than the national rate, not better.

Section one. At a glance.

New Jersey has 44 hospitals mandated to participate in the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services' Transforming Episode Accountability Model, known as TEAM, and every single one of them sits inside one Core-Based Statistical Area: New York, Newark, Jersey City. There's no upstate or rural New Jersey TEAM footprint the way there is in New York, because northern and central New Jersey are, economically and medically, part of the New York metro market. All 44 hospitals are mandatory participants, locked in for the model's full run through the end of 2030.

Section two. Where the mandate sits, and who it's shared with.

Because all 44 New Jersey hospitals sit in the same CBSA as 65 New York hospitals, this is functionally one 109-hospital market split across a state line. A patient from Bergen County might have surgery at a New Jersey hospital, recover at a New Jersey skilled nursing facility, and see a Manhattan specialist for follow-up, all inside one thirty-day TEAM episode. That shared geography does not mean shared target prices, Medicare still sets each hospital's target from its own historical spending. But it does mean New Jersey's 44 hospitals are drawing on the same limited pool of skilled nursing and home health capacity as New York's 65, all at the same time, all trying to bring episode costs in under target.

CMS does not publish hospital-level TEAM episode volumes, so this report does not estimate a New Jersey-specific episode count, unlike an earlier version of this briefing that did. That number would be invented, not measured.

Section three. Readmission performance where TEAM pays.

Now to the part of the analysis that actually predicts how these hospitals are likely to fare under the model: readmissions. This analysis matched all 44 New Jersey hospitals to Medicare's public readmissions file using their federal certification numbers, so the linkage is exact. All 44 have at least one reportable ratio in the current file, covering the period July 2021 through June 2024.

Here's the headline, and it's a real one, not a repeated error. Across every condition Medicare tracks, 62 percent of New Jersey's hospital-condition pairs exceed the excess readmission ratio threshold of one, against 48 percent nationally. That's a meaningful, genuine gap, and it holds up as the worst readmissions profile among every state with a large TEAM roster that this analysis checked. It's worth being precise about a claim that circulates calling New Jersey the single highest in the entire country: a few states with very small TEAM rosters, six or so hospitals each, post higher rates still, but a number computed from six hospitals is far noisier than one computed from 44. Among states where the number actually means something, New Jersey is the worst.

Where New Jersey differs from New York is that there's no favorable exception to point to on the surgical side. On hip and knee replacement, the procedure category TEAM weights most heavily, New Jersey's ratio runs above the national line: 56 percent of New Jersey's hip and knee pairs exceed the threshold, against 48 percent nationally. Pneumonia is the widest gap of all, 75 percent against 47 percent nationally. The one exception is coronary bypass, where New Jersey actually runs below the national rate, 40 percent against 50 percent, but bypass is a small slice of New Jersey's TEAM volume and it doesn't offset the pattern on hip and knee.

New Jersey is not an under-resourced state. Hackensack Meridian Health and RWJBarnabas Health are both large, well-capitalized systems with extensive post-acute partnerships, not understaffed community hospitals with no quality infrastructure. A few things could plausibly explain the gap anyway. First, density: a hundred and nine TEAM-mandated hospitals across New Jersey and New York are all competing for the same skilled nursing and home health capacity at once, which can push placements toward higher-cost options or push patients home before they're ready. Second, patient complexity in dense urban New Jersey counties is high, though Medicare's risk adjustment is designed to account for that. Neither of these is something this analysis can verify from the data available, they're offered as plausible explanations, not findings.

Section four. What this analysis does not estimate.

This report does not put a dollar figure on New Jersey's TEAM exposure. Medicare doesn't publish hospital-level target prices or episode volumes for TEAM, so any specific number circulating for New Jersey, including one an earlier version of this briefing used, is somebody's estimate, not a published fact. Optum Advisory projected in 2025 that 56 percent of TEAM hospitals nationally will lose money once downside risk begins, averaging 1.2 million dollars per facility. That's a national number. Given New Jersey's readmissions profile, it's reasonable to think the real New Jersey exposure runs higher than that national average, but this report doesn't attach a specific number to that intuition, because doing so without hospital-level data would be a guess dressed up as an estimate.

Section five. Considerations for hospital leadership.

If you run a New Jersey hospital in this program, a few things follow directly from the data. First, don't assume your system's overall resources or reputation predict your TEAM performance, pull your own hip and knee ratio specifically, since that's the category TEAM prices most heavily and it's exactly where New Jersey as a whole runs hot. Second, treat post-acute contracting as a competitive problem, not just an operational one, you are one of 109 hospitals across two states trying to place patients into the same limited set of skilled nursing and home health providers. Third, if your hospital is a safety-net facility, University Hospital in Newark or Bergen New Bridge specifically, look into Track 2 eligibility now, before Performance Year two begins. And fourth, use Performance Year one, the year with no downside risk, to actually fix discharge planning and post-acute routing, because the readmissions gap this analysis found is exactly the kind of problem that shows up as lost money once two-sided risk starts in 2027.

Section six. Methods and limitations.

Hospital counts and CBSA assignment come from the CMS TEAM participant list, matched by federal certification number. Readmission figures come from CMS's Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program dataset, joined to the same roster by certification number, covering the July 2021 through June 2024 performance period. This report does not model episode dollar amounts, target prices, or gain and loss projections for New Jersey specifically, because CMS does not publish the hospital-level data that would make such a model verifiable rather than invented. Use the written report for citations and the complete, live-generated hospital directory.

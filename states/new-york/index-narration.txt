CMS TEAM in New York: Seventy-Eight Mandated Hospitals, the Nation's Largest Bundled-Payment Market.

In brief. New York carries 78 hospitals in the Transforming Episode Accountability Model, the second-largest state roster in the country behind California, about 11 percent of the national list of 720. Every one of those 78 is mandatory, not voluntary. Sixty-five of them, 83 percent of the state, sit inside a single metropolitan market: New York, Newark, and Jersey City, the largest bundled-payment market in the country. On readmissions, the story that gets repeated about New York is mostly wrong. State hospitals run right at the national average overall, and on the two surgical categories TEAM actually pays for, hip and knee replacement and coronary bypass, New York hospitals are meaningfully better than the rest of the country, not worse.

Section one. At a glance.

New York has 78 hospitals mandated to participate in the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services' Transforming Episode Accountability Model, known as TEAM, spread across six Core-Based Statistical Areas. That's the second-largest state roster in the country, behind only California, and it's about 11 percent of the 720 hospitals on the entire national list. All 78 are mandatory participants; none joined voluntarily. Every one of them is locked in for the model's full run, January 1, 2026 through December 31, 2030.

TEAM itself is a real departure from how Medicare used to pay for surgery. The old model paid piece by piece: a fee for the operation, a separate fee for the hospital stay, and then whatever came after, billed and reimbursed on its own. TEAM collapses all of that into one thirty-day window. A hospital is now accountable, financially, for everything that happens to a patient for thirty days after five kinds of procedures: hip and knee replacement, spinal fusion, coronary bypass, surgical repair of a hip fracture, and major bowel surgery.

Section two. Where the mandate sits.

New York's exposure is not spread evenly across the state, it's overwhelmingly concentrated in one place. The New York, Newark, Jersey City metropolitan market accounts for 65 of the state's 78 hospitals, 83 percent of the total, and it's the single largest bundled-payment market in the entire country. That market also happens to be multi-state: it holds all of New Jersey's 44 mandated hospitals too, which means a patient discharged from a hospital in the Bronx and a patient discharged from a hospital in northern New Jersey are often drawing on the very same pool of skilled nursing facilities and home health agencies.

Outside the metro core, the roster thins out fast. Buffalo, Cheektowaga adds eight hospitals, about 10 percent of the state, anchored by Kaleida Health and the Erie County Medical Center. Four small upstate markets round out the list: Oneonta with two hospitals, and Batavia, Cortland, and Massena, Ogdensburg with one apiece. For those rural and critical-access facilities, the thirty-day episode window is a genuinely different problem than it is in New York City, because skilled nursing options are geographically limited and patients often travel a long way from their home county for surgical care in the first place.

The state's hospital market is also structurally unusual. Large nonprofit systems, Northwell Health, Mount Sinai, NYU Langone, NewYork-Presbyterian, and Montefiore, coexist with the city-run public hospital system, NYC Health and Hospitals, which includes Bellevue, Kings County, Lincoln, Elmhurst, Harlem, and several others. Those public hospitals serve a different patient population, more uninsured and dual-eligible patients, than the academic centers next door, which matters because their post-discharge resources and social risk factors are not the same going into a thirty-day accountability window.

Section three. Readmission performance where TEAM pays.

Now to the part of the analysis that actually predicts how these hospitals are likely to fare under the model: readmissions. Medicare publishes an excess readmission ratio for nearly every hospital in the country, condition by condition, comparing how many patients actually came back within thirty days against how many you'd expect given that hospital's case mix. A ratio under one means fewer readmissions than expected. Over one means more.

This analysis matched New York's TEAM hospitals to Medicare's public readmissions file using their federal certification numbers, so the linkage is exact. Seventy-five of the 78 have at least one reportable ratio in the current file. The data covers July 2021 through June 2024.

Here's the headline: across every condition Medicare tracks, 48.7 percent of New York's hospital-condition pairs exceed the excess readmission threshold, against 48.1 percent nationally. That is essentially the national average, not the meaningfully worse story that circulates about New York.

And when you split by condition, New York actually comes out ahead on the categories that matter most to TEAM. On hip and knee replacement, the procedure TEAM weights most heavily, New York's average ratio is 0.922 against 1.004 nationally, and only 24.4 percent of New York's hip and knee pairs exceed the threshold, against 47.9 percent nationally, essentially half the national rate. On coronary bypass, New York runs 0.931 against 1.002 nationally, with 31.6 percent of pairs above the line against 49.9 percent nationally. Those are not marginal gaps.

The weaker numbers sit entirely in the medical conditions Medicare also tracks under this program, conditions that fall outside TEAM's five surgical categories: heart attack, heart failure, pneumonia, and COPD. Heart attack in New York runs close to the national line, 0.997 against 1.002. Heart failure runs slightly hot, 1.012 against 1.001, with 48.6 percent of pairs above the line against 48.9 percent nationally, essentially even. Pneumonia is the widest gap: 1.020 against 1.001, with 54.8 percent of New York's pairs above the line against 46.8 percent nationally. COPD runs 1.014 against 1.001, with 57.6 percent above the line against 47.2 percent nationally.

Put plainly: on the readmission measure most directly relevant to TEAM, hip and knee replacement, New York's mandated hospitals meaningfully outperform the country. The state's readmissions profile only looks worse than average once you fold in medical conditions that TEAM doesn't pay for directly. A hospital in this state should not assume its overall HRRP profile predicts how it will do under TEAM specifically.

It's also worth flagging that a state average hides real variation underneath it. Even inside New York's strong hip and knee number, individual hospitals sit well above the line, Mount Sinai West and Mount Sinai South Nassau both post hip and knee ratios above 1.25, among the highest in the state's roster. For any individual hospital, the number that matters is its own ratio tied to its own federal certification number in Medicare's public file, not the state average.

Section four. What this analysis does not estimate.

One thing this report deliberately does not do is put a dollar figure on New York's TEAM exposure. Medicare does not publish hospital-level TEAM episode volumes or target prices in a form this analysis can verify, so any specific per-hospital or statewide dollar number you see elsewhere is somebody's estimate presented as fact, not a published figure. Two outside estimates are useful as general context only, not as New York-specific numbers. Optum Advisory projected in 2025 that 56 percent of TEAM-mandated hospitals nationally will lose money once downside risk begins, averaging 1.2 million dollars per facility, a national estimate whose underlying methodology isn't public. And the Healthcare Financial Management Association recommends hospitals use Performance Year one, the upside-only year, to build baseline episode cost data and post-acute partnerships before two-sided risk begins in Performance Year two, on January 1, 2027. A hospital that wants an actual New York-specific dollar estimate needs its own CMS-issued target price file, not a state-level average.

Section five. Considerations for hospital leadership.

So what should an executive in New York actually do with this. First, confirm your own hospital's status and performance using its federal certification number, not its name, since names drift across internal systems and Medicare's files. Second, don't assume your overall HRRP reputation predicts your TEAM performance, pull your own hip and knee and bypass ratios specifically, since those are the categories TEAM actually prices. Third, if your hospital is a safety-net facility inside the NYC Health and Hospitals system, look into Track 2 eligibility before Performance Year two begins, since the more forgiving stop-gain and stop-loss structure may reduce volatility for a patient population with different post-discharge needs. Fourth, treat the seven-day primary care follow-up requirement as an operational project now, not a Performance Year two problem, because primary care access in New York City is fragmented enough that this will take real lead time to build. And fifth, if you're one of the smaller upstate hospitals in Oneonta, Batavia, Cortland, or the Massena, Ogdensburg market, your post-acute network constraints are a different problem than the metro hospitals face, plan accordingly rather than importing a downstate playbook.

Section six. Methods and limitations.

Hospital counts and CBSA assignments in this report come from the CMS TEAM participant list, matched by federal certification number, so the linkage is exact rather than name-matched. Readmission figures come from CMS's Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program dataset, joined to the same roster by certification number, covering the July 2021 through June 2024 performance period. This report does not model episode dollar amounts, target prices, or gain and loss projections for New York specifically, because CMS does not publish the hospital-level data that would make such a model verifiable rather than invented. Use the written report for citations, the complete hospital directory by CBSA, and the full condition-by-condition readmissions table.

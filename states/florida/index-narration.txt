CMS TEAM in Florida: Forty Mandated Hospitals Across Eleven Markets, from Jacksonville to the Keys.

In brief. Florida carries 40 hospitals in the Transforming Episode Accountability Model, spread across 11 separate metropolitan markets, from Jacksonville in the northeast down to the Florida Keys. That geographic spread is real and unusual — no other major TEAM state is this dispersed. But the headline readmissions number that has previously circulated for Florida understates the real gap. This analysis found 61.4 percent of Florida's hospital-condition pairs exceed the excess readmission threshold, against 48.1 percent nationally — a bigger gap than previously claimed. And the story behind that number isn't the one the old version told, either. Florida does not have a favorable hip and knee replacement story, and it does not have an unfavorable one — the state sits close to national parity there. The real outlier is heart attack readmissions, where Florida's rate runs nearly one point seven times the national rate, the largest single-condition gap found in any state this analysis has checked.

Section one. At a glance.

Florida has 40 hospitals mandated to participate in the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services' Transforming Episode Accountability Model, known as TEAM, spread across 11 Core-Based Statistical Areas. Jacksonville is the largest single market, with 13 hospitals. Deltona-Daytona Beach follows with 8, dominated by AdventHealth. Crestview-Fort Walton Beach-Destin and Lakeland-Winter Haven each carry 5. The rest of the state's TEAM hospitals are spread thin: two each in Panama City and Punta Gorda, and single hospitals in markets as different as Key West, Lake City, Arcadia, and one hospital in the Miami-Fort Lauderdale-West Palm Beach CBSA, even though most of that metro area was not selected for TEAM at all.

Section two. Where the mandate sits.

Florida's TEAM footprint stretches more than 500 miles, from the Panhandle's Gulf Coast to the Florida Keys, and that distance matters operationally. Jacksonville's market includes an academic medical center in UF Health, a Mayo Clinic regional campus, and a four-hospital Ascension St. Vincent's network. Contrast that with Lower Keys Medical Center, the only hospital in Monroe County, serving a seasonal population with essentially no local skilled nursing options, where any institutional post-acute placement means a transfer to the mainland or to Miami-Dade. AdventHealth has the largest single-system footprint in the state, concentrated in the Deltona-Daytona Beach and Lakeland-Winter Haven clusters, with an additional hospital in Punta Gorda. HCA Florida spans the Panhandle and several smaller markets. CMS does not publish hospital-level Medicare population or episode-volume figures, so this report does not carry a Florida-specific beneficiary count or estimated episode volume the way an earlier version of this briefing did.

Section three. Readmission performance where TEAM pays.

This is where the real correction happens. This analysis matched Florida's TEAM roster to Medicare's public readmissions file by hospital certification number; 39 of the state's 40 mandated hospitals appear in that file, covering the period July 2021 through June 2024. Across every condition Medicare tracks, 61.4 percent of Florida's hospital-condition pairs exceed the excess readmission ratio threshold of one, against 48.1 percent nationally. That's a real, meaningful gap — and it's larger than the figure that has previously circulated for Florida, which undersold the actual story.

The "fifth-highest in the nation" ranking that goes along with that older number turns out to be roughly right, once every TEAM-participating state is checked, but it needs the same caveat this analysis applies elsewhere. A few states with very small TEAM rosters — Indiana with 6 hospitals, Georgia with 13, Missouri with 6 — post higher pooled rates than Florida, but those numbers are noisier, computed from a handful of hospitals each. Among states with a large, meaningful TEAM roster, Florida's 61.4 percent lands just behind New Jersey's, which makes Florida the second-worst readmissions profile of any large-roster TEAM state this analysis has checked.

Here's the part that actually changes the story. The old version of this briefing pointed to hip and knee replacement as the condition driving Florida's number, calling it the most important condition for TEAM and citing its high average readmission rate as an explanation. That's a general fact about hip and knee replacement nationally — it isn't what's happening in Florida specifically. Florida's hip and knee readmission rate is 45 percent of pairs above threshold, against 47.9 percent nationally — essentially at parity, not a gap in either direction. The condition that actually stands out is heart attack care: 82.8 percent of Florida's heart-attack hospital-condition pairs exceed the threshold, against 49.7 percent nationally. That's not just the worst number in this report — it's the largest single-condition gap this analysis has found in any state checked so far, roughly one point seven times the national rate. Florida also runs meaningfully worse than the national rate on coronary bypass, 68.8 percent against 49.9; on heart failure, 56.8 against 48.9; and on COPD, 68.6 against 47.2. Pneumonia is close to parity, 48.7 against 46.8.

Section four. What this analysis does not estimate.

An earlier version of this briefing estimated a specific annual count of lower-extremity joint replacement episodes across Florida's TEAM hospitals, and projected a multi-million-dollar aggregate savings figure from a hypothetical shift from skilled nursing to home health after discharge. Neither of those figures traces to any CMS data this analysis can verify, so this report does not repeat them. Optum Advisory projected in 2025 that 56 percent of TEAM hospitals nationally will lose money once downside risk begins, averaging 1.2 million dollars per facility — a national number, not a Florida-specific one. Given Florida's readmissions profile, especially on heart attack care, it's reasonable to think the real Florida exposure runs higher than that national average, but this report doesn't attach a specific number to that intuition. It's also worth noting that the leading evidence base for post-acute cost reduction, the Dummit and Finkelstein studies this report cites elsewhere, is specific to joint replacement — the one condition where Florida is closest to national parity. That's not necessarily the right lever for Florida's actual outlier finding on heart attack readmissions, which is a medical condition with a different post-discharge pathway than a surgical one.

Section five. Considerations for hospital leadership.

If you run a Florida hospital in this program, a few things follow directly from the data. First, don't assume your hip and knee replacement readmission rate is where your TEAM risk concentrates — for Florida as a whole it isn't, and your own hospital's number may or may not track the statewide picture. Second, look specifically at your heart attack readmission rate, since that's where Florida's real outlier sits, and it's not a condition TEAM's core procedure category was designed around. Third, if you're in one of Florida's smaller or more rural markets — the Keys, Arcadia, the Panhandle — factor in that your post-acute options are more geographically constrained than a Jacksonville or Tampa hospital's, and CMS's risk adjustment only partially accounts for that. Fourth, use Performance Year one, the year with no downside risk, to dig into your own hospital-level HRRP numbers by condition rather than relying on the statewide average, because the pattern that matters for your hospital may look nothing like the state's overall profile.

Section six. Methods and limitations.

Hospital counts and CBSA assignment come from the CMS TEAM participant list, matched by federal certification number. Readmission figures come from CMS's Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program dataset, joined to the same roster by certification number, covering the July 2021 through June 2024 performance period; 39 of Florida's 40 mandated hospitals appear in that file. This report does not model episode dollar amounts, target prices, or gain and loss projections for Florida specifically, because CMS does not publish the hospital-level data that would make such a model verifiable rather than invented. Use the written report for citations and the complete, live-generated hospital directory by CBSA.
